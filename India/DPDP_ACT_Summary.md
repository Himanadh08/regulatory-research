## 1. Overview

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 (DPDP Act) is India's first comprehensive 
data protection legislation, receiving Presidential assent on August 11, 2023. It governs 
the processing of digital personal data within India and establishes rights for individuals 
(Data Principals) and obligations for organisations processing their data (Data Fiduciaries).

The DPDP Act draws significant influence from the EU's General Data Protection Regulation 
(GDPR) while being structured for India's regulatory and economic context. It replaces the 
data protection provisions previously embedded in the Information Technology Act 2000.

**Regulator:** Data Protection Board of India (yet to be constituted as of mid-2026)  
**Enforcement:** Rules under the Act are pending finalisation — organisations are preparing 
compliance frameworks in anticipation

---

## 2. Key Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Data Principal | The individual whose personal data is being processed |
| Data Fiduciary | Any entity that determines the purpose and means of processing personal data |
| Significant Data Fiduciary | A Data Fiduciary designated by the government based on volume, sensitivity, or risk |
| Personal Data | Any data about an identifiable individual |
| Data Processor | Entity that processes data on behalf of a Data Fiduciary |
| Consent Manager | A platform through which individuals can manage their consents |
| Data Protection Board | The regulatory body established under the Act to adjudicate complaints and impose penalties |

---

## 3. Scope of the Act

**Applies to:**
- Processing of digital personal data within India
- Processing outside India if it involves offering goods or services to individuals in India

**Does NOT apply to:**
- Personal data processed for personal or domestic purposes
- Personal data made publicly available by the Data Principal themselves
- Certain government exemptions for national security and law enforcement

---

## 4. Rights of Data Principals

| Right | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| Right to information | Know what personal data is being processed and why |
| Right to correction | Request correction of inaccurate or incomplete data |
| Right to erasure | Request deletion of personal data when no longer necessary |
| Right to grievance redressal | Raise complaints with the Data Fiduciary and escalate to Data Protection Board |
| Right to nominate | Nominate another individual to exercise rights in case of death or incapacity |

**Notable difference from GDPR:** DPDP Act does not include a right to data portability 
or a right to object to processing — these exist in GDPR but are absent here.

---

## 5. Obligations of Data Fiduciaries

### 5.1 Consent Requirements
- Consent must be free, specific, informed, unconditional, and unambiguous
- Must be given through a clear affirmative action
- Must be as easy to withdraw as to give
- A notice must be provided before seeking consent explaining what data is collected 
  and for what purpose

### 5.2 Purpose Limitation
- Personal data can only be processed for the purpose for which consent was given
- Cannot be retained beyond what is necessary for that purpose

### 5.3 Data Minimisation
- Only collect data that is necessary for the specified purpose
- No excessive collection beyond what the purpose requires

### 5.4 Security Safeguards
- Implement reasonable security measures to prevent personal data breaches
- Notify the Data Protection Board and affected Data Principals in case of a breach

### 5.5 Grievance Officer
- Every Data Fiduciary must designate a grievance officer
- Must respond to individual complaints within a defined timeframe

---

## 6. Significant Data Fiduciaries — Additional Obligations

Organisations designated as Significant Data Fiduciaries by the government face 
enhanced obligations:

- Appoint a **Data Protection Officer** (must be based in India)
- Appoint an independent **data auditor**
- Conduct periodic **Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIA)**
- Comply with additional government-notified requirements

---

## 7. Cross-Border Data Transfers

The DPDP Act allows transfer of personal data to countries or territories notified 
by the Central Government as permissible destinations. The specific list of permitted 
countries has not been finalised as of mid-2026. This is a significant pending element 
for organisations with international data flows.

**Contrast with GDPR:** GDPR uses adequacy decisions and Standard Contractual Clauses 
for cross-border transfers — a more detailed and prescriptive framework than the DPDP 
Act's government-notified country approach.

---

## 8. Penalties

| Violation | Maximum Penalty |
|---|---|
| Failure to implement security safeguards leading to a breach | Rs. 250 crore |
| Failure to notify breach to Board or Data Principal | Rs. 200 crore |
| Non-compliance with provisions related to children's data | Rs. 200 crore |
| Non-compliance by Significant Data Fiduciary | Rs. 150 crore |
| Other violations | Rs. 50 crore |

Penalties are adjudicated by the Data Protection Board — not courts — making the 
Board a quasi-judicial authority with significant enforcement power.

---

## 9. Children's Data — Special Provisions

- Verifiable parental consent required before processing data of individuals under 18
- Data Fiduciaries cannot process children's data in ways that cause detrimental 
  effects on their wellbeing
- Behavioural tracking and targeted advertising directed at children is prohibited

---

## 10. DPDP vs GDPR — Key Comparison

| Aspect | DPDP Act (India) | GDPR (EU) |
|---|---|---|
| Lawful bases for processing | Primarily consent + certain legitimate uses | 6 lawful bases including legitimate interest |
| Right to portability | Not included | Included |
| Right to object | Not included | Included |
| Cross-border transfers | Government-notified countries | Adequacy decisions + SCCs |
| DPO requirement | Only for Significant Data Fiduciaries | Mandatory for certain categories |
| Penalty structure | Up to Rs.250 crore | Up to 4% of global annual turnover |
| Regulator | Data Protection Board (quasi-judicial) | Individual EU member state supervisory authorities |
| Extraterritorial scope | Yes — offering services to Indians | Yes — offering services to EU residents |
| Children's age threshold | Under 18 | Under 16 (varies by member state, min 13) |

## 11. GRC Implications — What This Means for Organisations

### Immediate compliance actions organisations should take:
- Conduct a **data inventory** — map all personal data collected, where it is stored, 
  how it is used, and who has access
- Review and update **privacy notices** to meet DPDP consent requirements
- Establish a **consent management mechanism** — especially critical for digital 
  platforms with large user bases
- Designate a **Grievance Officer** and publish contact details
- Review **data retention policies** — ensure data is deleted when no longer necessary
- Prepare a **breach notification procedure** — the Act requires notification to both 
  the Board and affected individuals

### Sectors most impacted:
- Banking and financial services (large volumes of sensitive financial data)
- Healthcare (patient data, medical records)
- E-commerce and retail (purchase history, behaviour tracking)
- EdTech (children's data — highest risk category)
- Telecom (location data, call records)

## 12. Current Implementation Status (as of mid-2026)

- DPDP Act received Presidential assent: August 11, 2023
- Draft rules for the Act: under consultation, not yet finalised
- Data Protection Board: not yet constituted
- Most organisations in pre-compliance preparation phase — building internal frameworks 
  before formal enforcement begins
- Expected enforcement timeline: dependent on rules finalisation and Board constitution
